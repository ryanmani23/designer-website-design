We are roofing snobs, and we will say so plainly. Given the choice and the budget, we install natural slate. But we also install synthetic slate, and we install it without apology when it is the right answer. That puts us in a small group: contractors who sell both, and therefore have no reason to oversell either.

So here is the honest comparison, the one we give clients across the table before anyone signs anything.

## What each one actually is

**Natural slate** is stone. It is split from the earth, graded, and laid by hand. It has been roofing buildings for centuries and it will keep doing it for centuries more. Nothing else on this list is stone.

**Synthetic slate** is a manufactured tile engineered to look like slate. The good ones are molded from real slate so the texture is genuine, not stamped plastic. The category includes composite products and polymer products, and the quality range is enormous. Most of what is sold as "synthetic slate" we will not install. Two products we will: Brava and DaVinci.

That distinction matters more than the natural-versus-synthetic question itself. A bad synthetic slate is a bad roof. A good one is a genuinely excellent roof that happens to cost less than stone.

## Lifespan

Natural slate wins, and it is not close. A hard Vermont slate roof will run a century or more. The best synthetics carry warranties in the 50-year range and will likely perform beyond that, but they have not been on roofs for 150 years because they have not existed for 150 years.

For most homeowners, this distinction is smaller than it sounds. A 50-year roof installed at 45 is a roof you never replace. The lifespan gap only becomes the deciding factor on a true heirloom property, the kind meant to stay in one family across generations, where a century of service is the point.

## Weight and structure

This is where synthetic earns its keep. Natural slate is heavy. A slate roof can require structural reinforcement on a home that was not framed for it, and that adds real cost and complexity.

Synthetic slate is a fraction of the weight. On a re-roof where the structure was built for asphalt or for a lighter tile, a composite slate can deliver the look of stone without touching the framing. On new construction designed for the load, this advantage disappears, and the question becomes purely one of material.

## Looks

From the ground, the best synthetics are convincing. From ten feet away, on a roof you stand under every day, the eye eventually knows. Natural slate has a depth and an irregularity that comes from being stone, and a variation across the field that a mold repeats and stone never does.

We say this as people who care about the difference. Most guests at your dinner party will not. Whether that depth is worth the premium is a question only the homeowner can answer, and it is a fair question to answer either way.

## Impact and hail

Here the synthetics have a real, measurable edge that matters in Texas. The composite slates we install carry a Class 4 impact rating, the highest there is. They are built to take hail that would crack natural stone.

Natural slate is repairable tile by tile, which is its own kind of resilience, but a Class 4 synthetic in a hail corridor is a legitimate argument, and an honest one. This is the single strongest case for going synthetic on a DFW roof, and we make it to clients regularly.

## Cost

Natural slate is one of the most expensive roofing systems in the world, between the material, the weight, and the craft required to lay it. Synthetic slate costs meaningfully less while still sitting well above asphalt and most other options. It is a premium roof at a price below stone.

We will not pretend either one is cheap. Neither is. Both are built to be the last roof the house needs for a very long time, and both are priced accordingly.

## So which one

There is no universal answer, which is exactly why we carry both. The shape of the decision usually looks like this:

- **Choose natural slate** when the home is historic or estate-class, the structure can carry the weight or will be built to, and the goal is a roof that outlives everyone who argues about it.
- **Choose synthetic slate** when weight is a constraint, when a Class 4 hail rating is a priority, or when you want the character of stone at a cost that leaves room in the budget for the rest of the house.

What we will not do is install a synthetic slate we do not believe in, or talk a client into stone they do not need. We only install what we would put on our own homes. The conversation is about which of those two belongs on yours.

If you are weighing slate against composite for a home in the DFW area, we will walk the roof, look at the structure, and give you the recommendation we would give family.
