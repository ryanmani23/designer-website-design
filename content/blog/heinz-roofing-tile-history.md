Some roofing tile companies built their reputation on volume. The Heinz Roofing Tile Company of Denver took a different route: betting on craftsmanship, texture, and the illusion of age, while its founder quietly became one of the more interesting patrons of the arts in early Denver.

## From Sales Rep to Founder

George P. Heinz (1872-1932) started his career in the tile business in Denver as a sales representative for Ludowici Tile. At some point he decided to manufacture tile himself, founding the Heinz Roofing Tile Company in 1912 and drawing predominantly on local clay from the area around Golden, Colorado. Heinz recognized that tile needed to be more than durable; it needed to be artistic, and he pushed to develop colors and glazes that weren't otherwise available in the early 1900s, timing his push to the boom in tile roofs on Spanish Revival and Tudor-style homes.

The company wasn't small. By 1927, the plant ran five fully operational kilns, consuming 24 tons of clay a day. Heinz took on custom, architecturally demanding projects where tile had to be made to fit the building rather than the other way around. He was also civic-minded enough to serve as a trustee of the Denver Art Museum (then housed at the Chappell House) from 1925 to 1931.

## Selling "Old Country" Character, Not Just Coverage

Heinz's signature line was the "Old English Plymouth Tile," a shingle-style tile designed to mimic the softened, weathered look of centuries-old English and northern French roofs, tile the company said its researchers studied firsthand by traveling abroad to collect samples. Rather than machine-produced uniformity, the company marketed the opposite: no two tiles alike, deliberately roughened surfaces, and a rain-beaten texture applied by hand in multiple stages.

Ordinary clays tended to fire with a shine that gave away a tile's modern origin, so Heinz turned to Colorado shale, which could be fired at high temperature without vitrifying, keeping the surface matte rather than glossy. Plymouth Tile shipped in fifteen color groups, blending tones like burgundy, lilac, gray-green, straw yellow, and smudge black within a single shingle, letting homeowners and architects choose a cast, greens and grays, or browns and purples, suited to the building.

Beyond Plymouth Tile, Heinz also produced Spanish, Dutch, Dorian, and Italian profiles, each researched from period examples abroad. Installations ranged across the U.S. and Canada, including the Denver home of Mr. and Mrs. Churchill Owens, designed by the architecture firm Hoyt and Hoyt.

## An Unlikely Artistic Collaboration

Heinz's plant on the north end of downtown Denver became, for a couple of years in the late 1920s, an unlikely meeting point between commercial tile-making and fine art. In 1927, Heinz hired ceramics instructor Paul Saint-Gaudens, nephew of the sculptor Augustus Saint-Gaudens, who was teaching at Denver's Chappell School. Saint-Gaudens used the kilns at Heinz's plant to produce his own decorative tile work, often signed with his initials, drawing heavily on Mayan design influences.

Sometime around 1928, the well-known San Ildefonso Pueblo artist Awa Tsireh (Alfonso Roybal) also appears to have worked at the Heinz plant, likely through a connection made in Santa Fe involving Saint-Gaudens. Tiles bearing Awa Tsireh's hand-painted and molded designs, many stamped "Geo P Heinz" on the reverse, surfaced decades later in a rediscovered Colorado collection, and several examples had already been incorporated into the Garden of the Gods Trading Post's 1929 construction in Manitou Springs. It's a small footnote in the company's history, but a striking one: a Denver roofing tile factory briefly became a working studio for two very different artists.

## The Company's End

Heinz Roofing Tile Company closed in 1945. Its tile has outlasted it, though: suppliers who stock historic clay tile for roof repair list "Heinz Spanish" profile tile among their reclaimed inventory, and custom tile fabricators reference matching "Heinz and Ludowici" shingle slabs and hip rolls when reproducing period roofs, a sign that Heinz roofs, wherever they still stand, are being kept alive tile by tile rather than replaced wholesale.

Matching a Heinz roof today means salvage or remanufacture rather than a call to the factory, since Ludowici can still reproduce discontinued Heinz molds. It is exactly the kind of [discontinued-tile sourcing](discontinued.html) we handle on estate roofs across North Texas.
