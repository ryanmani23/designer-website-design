Long before vinyl siding and asphalt shingles took over the American roofline, clay tile was the mark of a building meant to last. And for over half a century, one of the names behind that durability was the Mound City Roofing Tile Company of St. Louis, Missouri.

## A Name Rooted in St. Louis

St. Louis has long carried the nickname "Mound City," a nod to the large earthen mounds built by Indigenous peoples of the Mississippian culture, who once populated the Mississippi River Valley. Early settlers encountered these mounds scattered throughout the region, and the name stuck to the city itself, and, eventually, to the businesses within it. The Mound City Roofing Tile Company was one of many local companies to adopt the name as a point of civic pride.

## Built for the Boom

The company was founded in 1903, a banner year for the roofing tile industry. That same year saw the construction of two other major plants, the United States Roofing Tile Company in Parkersburg, West Virginia, and the Western Roofing Tile Company in Coffeyville, Kansas. St. Louis, in particular, was already a hub for clay tile manufacturing; by 1900, the city had become the second-largest producer of roofing tile in the country, a legacy that traced back to the first clay tile manufactured in the U.S. by a St. Louis company in the 1860s. Mound City wasn't even the first roofing tile plant in town; the Standard Roofing Tile Company had been operating in St. Louis since 1895, giving Mound City an established local competitor from day one. Nationally, the industry was still young: the Ludowici Roofing Tile Company had only built its Georgia plant in 1900, and the Murray Roofing Tile Company (Cloverport, Kentucky) and Detroit Roofing Tile Company wouldn't be founded until 1904 and 1906, respectively.

Mound City's plant was located at 3301-9 Morgan Ford Road. Fittingly, the office building, machinery building, and an adjoining two-story structure were all built using the company's own hollow vitrified tile, making it a working advertisement for the product line, visible on the very roofs of the plant itself.

## From Shale to Shingle

A 1911 write-up in Brick and Clay Record gives a detailed look at how the tile was actually made. Shale was hauled in from Malcolm Station, thirteen miles from the plant, then left to "sweat" for at least two weeks before grinding. From there, it went through a dry pan, a pug mill, and finally into clay bins, kept on a rotating four-day cycle so the material was never used before it had properly aged.

Once ground and pugged, the clay was pressed into shape using revolving presses (for the standard interlocking tile) and trimming presses (for crestings, hip rolls, starters, and specialty pieces). The plant also ran an auger machine for shingle tile and Imperial Spanish tile. At full capacity, Mound City could turn out 10,000 interlocking tiles a day or a mix of shingle and Spanish tile in similar volumes.

After pressing, tile spent 30 hours in tunnel dryers before heading into the kilns for a 36-to-40-hour burn. The company fired its kilns entirely with fuel oil rather than coal, a choice that, according to the trade write-up, gave Mound City tile a notably consistent, rich red color and largely eliminated the discolored "flashed" tile that plagued less careful producers.

By 1911, the company's reach was genuinely national: shipments went out from Maine to California, and from Minnesota to Texas.

## What They Made

Mound City's catalogs, a 1911 "Catalog D" and a 1915 "Catalogue E," both preserved today by the National Building Arts Center, show a product line broader than a single signature tile. Offerings included:

- French A Tile and French A Half Tile, an interlocking style
- B Tile, another interlocking profile
- Panama Tile
- C Spanish Tile and S Spanish Tile
- Matching gable copings, terminals, finials, and ornamental crestings for slate roofs

This range let the company outfit everything from modest interlocking roofs to the Mediterranean-style Spanish tile popular on early-20th-century homes.

## The Company's End

The Mound City Roofing Tile Company did not have a single precisely recorded closing date, but historical records indicate the St. Louis manufacturer went out of business sometime during the 1920s. What we do know is that the tile itself outlasted the company: restoration suppliers like Tile Roofs Inc. still stock and custom-match "Mound City French tile" today for homeowners repairing historic roofs.

## A Legacy Still on the Roof

Though much of these roofs were just replaced in the 2025 tornado that hit St. Louis, discontinued or not, Mound City tile is still doing its job. A century-old roof holding its original Mound City tile is a quiet testament to the company's fuel-oil kilns and four-day aging cycle. Simply proof that some 1903 engineering decisions are still paying off today.
