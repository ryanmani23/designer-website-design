Few names in American building materials carry as much continuous institutional memory as Ludowici. The company operating today under that name traces an unbroken, if often turbulent, line back to a mid-19th-century German tileworks, through Gilded Age Chicago, a Georgia company town, a New York ceramics college, and four separate corporate parents, arriving in the present day as the oldest continuously operating clay roof tile manufacturer in the United States. Its tile sits atop the White House, the Pennsylvania State Capitol, and Wrigley Field, and its own factory complex in New Lexington, Ohio, is now itself a listed historic site. This is a history of a building material company that has, over roughly a century and a half, become inseparable from the buildings it was hired to protect.

## Origins in the German Palatinate

Ludowici traces its roots back nearly four centuries to a Ludovisi family in Rome, whose skill in crafting clay tile was well regarded before the family moved north, settling in Germany and Germanizing the family name to Ludowici. The best-documented chapter of that German story begins in 1857, when Carl Ludowici, a machinist in the town of Ensheim in the German Palatinate, purchased a local roof tile factory and began upgrading it with machinery of his own design. He founded the firm Carl Ludowici Ziegelwerke, and in 1861 relocated it to a new factory in Ludwigshafen, on the Rhine. The company's growth in this period owed much to a specific technical innovation: Ludowici's steam-powered tile press, which allowed for far greater and more consistent output than the hand methods still common among European tile makers.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1">1</a>, <a href="#src-8">8</a></sup>

Carl Ludowici died in 1881. His sons, Wilhelm and Franz, took over the firm, dividing responsibilities along lines that would recur throughout the company's history: Franz managed the business side, while Wilhelm led design and product development. Under their direction the company relocated its primary operations to Jockgrim, where a large ring kiln, ninety meters long with forty-six firing chambers, could produce roughly 21,000 tiles in a single day-long firing cycle. Fired continuously on hard coal, the Jockgrim works became one of the major tile manufacturers in Germany, eventually operating as many as twelve branches across France, Germany, and the United States by 1935. Its tile reached some of Europe's most prominent rooftops, including St. Stephen's Cathedral in Vienna and later the Olympic Village built for the 1936 Berlin Olympics. The German operation persisted for decades after its American offshoot had grown far larger, and did not close until 1972, when the local clay deposits that had supplied it since the Roman era, according to local historians, were considered exhausted.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1">1</a></sup>

## Two Parallel American Beginnings

The American history of Ludowici tile is, somewhat unusually, the product of two originally unrelated ventures that only later merged.

The first began in 1888 in Alfred, New York, a small village that was, and remains, home to Alfred University. A sculpting professor there determined that the local clay was well suited to ornamental work, and organized local investors to found the Celadon Terra Cotta Company, its name drawn from the pale green hue the clay took on when salt-fired, a color historically associated with Chinese ceramics. Among the early investors was George Herman Babcock, better known today as co-inventor of the Babcock & Wilcox boiler; Babcock became president of Celadon and personally filed patents for several tile profiles, including the Conosera tile and a system of combination tiles that let multiple interlocking designs share a common base, allowing several styles to be combined on a single roof. Babcock died in 1893, but the company continued to grow, shifting its focus toward roofing tile and renaming itself the Celadon Roofing Tile Company in 1900. Celadon's presence in Alfred was significant enough that it helped push for the establishment of the New York State School of Clay-Working and Ceramics at Alfred University, a program that gave the company access to leading contemporary ceramicists, including consulting work from Charles Fergus Binns, a foundational figure in American studio ceramics.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-5">5</a></sup>

The second American venture ran in parallel and carried the Ludowici name directly. In 1893, the German Ludowici firm licensed its patents and designs to a newly formed Ludowici Roofing Tile Company based in Chicago. That year the new company exhibited tile at the World's Columbian Exposition, using the fair to introduce German-engineered tile designs to an American audience, and built a factory in Chicago Heights that grew, by the turn of the century, into one of the leading roofing tile producers in the country. In 1902 the company built a further factory in the unincorporated community of Liberty City, Georgia; the town's residents were sufficiently grateful for the company's local philanthropy that Liberty City was formally incorporated as Ludowici, Georgia, in 1905, a rare instance of an American town renaming itself after a roofing tile manufacturer. Over the following years the Georgia plant supplied more than two million square feet of roofing tile for government buildings constructed in the Panama Canal Zone, a contract substantial enough to sustain the factory through a period when regional demand alone would not have. When that federal work concluded, demand fell off sharply enough that the Ludowici, Georgia plant closed in October 1913. The timing proved unusually harsh: the following month, the company's Chicago Heights factory burned to the ground and was never rebuilt, leaving the Ludowici Roofing Tile Company with no operating American factory of its own at all.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1">1</a>, <a href="#src-7">7</a></sup>

## Merger and Consolidation

The two companies' fortunes intertwined well before that crisis. In 1905, the Celadon Company purchased the Imperial Clay Company, gaining a factory built three years earlier in New Lexington, Ohio, by Wolsey Garnet Worcester. In 1906, Celadon and the Ludowici Roofing Tile Company formally merged to form the Ludowici-Celadon Company, combining the German patents and Ludowici name with Celadon's New York ceramics expertise and Ohio manufacturing capacity. The merged company purchased a further plant in Coffeyville, Kansas, in 1908.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-5">5</a></sup>

The consolidation proved fortunate in timing. In 1909, the original Celadon factory in Alfred, New York, burned to the ground; the company chose not to rebuild there, and the village's tile-making era effectively ended, though the original Celadon office building survived the fire and stands in Alfred today, listed on the National Register of Historic Places. When the Chicago Heights and Ludowici, Georgia plants were lost in quick succession in 1913, the merged company still had its New Lexington and Coffeyville factories intact, and those two sites became, and largely remained, Ludowici-Celadon's entire American manufacturing base for the following decades.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1">1</a></sup>

## The Interwar Architectural Boom

Ludowici-Celadon's fortunes rose through the first quarter of the twentieth century, buoyed by a widespread architectural enthusiasm for Mediterranean and Spanish Revival styles that depended heavily on terra cotta tile roofing, a taste visible in university campuses (including the University of Texas at Austin, whose Spanish-tile buildings were supplied substantially by Ludowici alongside Texas-made competitors) and in prominent residential and civic architecture nationwide. In 1929 the company moved to capture and cultivate that enthusiasm directly, publishing a series called The Tuileries Brochures, illustrated booklets that combined product promotion with substantive writing on traditional regional building styles by prominent architects and writers of the day, including Aymar Embury II, Frederick Ackerman, Jacques Carlu, and Hilaire Belloc. The brochures functioned less as a catalog than as an architectural argument for the material, an early example of a building products company positioning itself as a custodian of design culture rather than a mere supplier.

## Depression, War, and Contraction

The Second World War hit Ludowici-Celadon's core business hard. Domestic construction slowed sharply, and demand for architectural terra cotta, always somewhat discretionary compared to structural building materials, declined with it. The company responded by temporarily diversifying both its New Lexington and Coffeyville plants into pottery production, using existing kilns and clay-working expertise to manufacture goods with more immediate wartime demand, including, notably, licensed cookie jars produced under agreement with Walt Disney, an unlikely intersection of roofing tile manufacturing and mid-century American pop culture.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1">1</a>, <a href="#src-8">8</a></sup>

The postwar recovery in domestic construction did not fully restore the terra cotta tile market to its earlier scale, and the industry-wide shift toward cheaper roofing materials continued to squeeze demand. In 1956, the Coffeyville, Kansas, factory was closed, a direct consequence of the declining market for terra cotta tile nationally, leaving the New Lexington, Ohio, plant as Ludowici-Celadon's sole remaining production site, a position it has held ever since.

## Corporate Transitions: 1976 to the Present

Ludowici-Celadon changed corporate ownership several times over the following half-century, each transition reflecting broader consolidation trends in American building materials manufacturing. In 1976, the company was acquired by CSC Inc. of Chicago. The 1980s brought an unexpected revival: a growing national interest in historic preservation and restoration created new demand for authentic architectural terra cotta, and in 1986 Ludowici-Celadon sponsored a competition and exhibition with the National Building Museum focused on architectural terra cotta ornamentation, a sign of the company's success at repositioning itself within the emerging historic preservation movement.

In 1989, CSC sold Ludowici-Celadon to CertainTeed, a subsidiary of the French building materials conglomerate Saint-Gobain. CertainTeed shortened the company's name to Ludowici Roof Tile in 1994, dropping the Celadon half of its identity after nearly ninety years. Around 2002, management passed within Saint-Gobain's corporate structure to another subsidiary, Terreal; when Terreal itself spun off from Saint-Gobain as an independent company in 2003, Ludowici went with it.

The 2000s and 2010s saw Ludowici expand its product range into wall cladding tile and invest in its public-facing presence, opening a first showroom in 2007 in a renovated shipping building at the New Lexington factory, followed by a larger showroom in Dallas, Texas, in 2019 aimed at the architect and design community. In 2021, the historic core of the New Lexington factory complex, the Ludowici Roof Tile Company Historic District, was added to the National Register of Historic Places, formally recognizing the site's industrial architecture (much of it, fittingly, designed by Wolsey Garnet Worcester) alongside its continued, active manufacturing use. Most recently, in 2024, Terreal and its subsidiaries were acquired by the Austrian building materials group Wienerberger, and management of Ludowici transitioned to General Shale, another Wienerberger-owned American subsidiary, the company's fourth corporate parent since 1976.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1">1</a>, <a href="#src-6">6</a>, <a href="#src-9">9</a></sup>

## Manufacturing and Technical Character

Much of what distinguishes Ludowici tile technically traces back to innovations made across both its German and American histories: Carl Ludowici's steam-powered press in the 1860s, which first made large-scale, consistent tile production commercially viable; the fire-flashing kiln process, which produces the mottled, individually varied color characteristic of much historic terra cotta roofing by deliberately limiting oxygen during firing; and, in 1991, the installation of North America's first hydrocasing kiln at New Lexington, replacing the older coal-fired brick kilns and modernizing production while preserving the traditional forming methods. This combination of nineteenth-century design lineage and selectively modernized manufacturing is a large part of why Ludowici tile remains legible, and matchable, on buildings a century or more old.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-3">3</a>, <a href="#src-4">4</a></sup>

## Architectural Legacy

Ludowici tile's presence across prominent American architecture is difficult to overstate. It roofs sections of the White House, the dome of the Pennsylvania State Capitol, New York's Plaza Hotel, and the New York State Capitol's corner towers. A particularly distinctive commission saw Ludowici tile gold-plated for use on the New York Life Building. The company's tile covers the restored facade of Wrigley Field, the Germany Pavilion at EPCOT and other structures throughout Walt Disney World, and the dome of the Basilica of the National Shrine of the Immaculate Conception in Washington, D.C. This range, spanning government buildings, luxury hotels, ballparks, theme parks, and religious architecture, reflects both the material's genuine versatility and the company's long institutional relationships with architects and preservation professionals across very different building types.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-2">2</a>, <a href="#src-10">10</a></sup>

## Conclusion

Ludowici's history resists a single tidy narrative arc. It is at once a story of German industrial innovation transplanted to America, of two independent companies merging into a stronger whole, of near-total destruction by fire not once but twice within a few years, of wartime improvisation into cookie jars, and of repeated absorption into ever-larger multinational building materials conglomerates without ever losing its own name or its single Ohio manufacturing site. What holds constant across all of it is the New Lexington plant itself, continuously operating in some form since 1902, and a body of tile scattered across the country's most recognizable rooftops that has, in its own quiet way, outlasted every company that has owned the right to make it.

That unbroken manufacturing lineage is not just a matter of historical interest. It is the reason a Ludowici roof can still be sourced, matched, and remade today, and why Ludowici can reproduce the profiles of long-gone makers like Heinz and B. Mifflin Hood. For homeowners in the DFW area with a Ludowici roof that needs matching or repair, it is exactly the history that makes the tile [worth saving rather than replacing](discontinued.html).
