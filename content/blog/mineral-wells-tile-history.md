Not every historic tile maker built its name in a big city. The Mineral Wells Tile Company took its name from the small Palo Pinto County, Texas town it was based in, a town better known at the time for its healing mineral springs than for building materials, and its tile ended up on some of the most photographed rooftops in the state.

## The Town Behind the Tile

Mineral Wells, Texas built its early identity as a health resort, drawing visitors to its "Crazy Water" mineral springs starting in the 1880s. But the same ground that made the town famous for water also turned out to hold good clay. By the turn of the 20th century, Palo Pinto County's clay deposits were recognized as suitable for brick and tile manufacturing, and the area's coal mines supplied the fuel to fire it, using rejected fine coal that wasn't good for much else. A large paving brick plant went up in Mineral Wells in 1921 and, by 1927, was reportedly the largest of its kind west of the Mississippi. Mineral Wells Tile Company operated in this same clay-rich environment, though it was a distinct operation from the brick plants and shale suppliers also working the area's clay.

## Manufacturing and Characteristics

Mineral Wells tile was fired in old bee-hive kilns using local Texas shale and clay, a process that gave the tile a natural, broad color variation rather than a single uniform tone. The company produced at least two documented profiles: a 15-inch by 7-inch flat slab shingle tile, and a directional-scored Pan and Cover tile (also called Mission tile), a two-piece Spanish-style system in which a rounded "cover" tile overlaps a curved "pan" tile beneath it.

The bee-hive kiln process meant Mineral Wells tile came out "fire flashed," with kiln heat unevenly coloring individual tiles across a range from soft buffs to deep browns to earthy reds within the same batch. That variation, the same effect described on Ludowici and other manufacturers' fire-flashed tile of the era, is part of what gives historic Mineral Wells roofs their mottled, weathered look today.

## Notable Installations

Mineral Wells tile made it onto some significant Texas roofs. At the University of Texas at Austin, the tiled roofs of the Main Building, Painter Hall, and Hogg Memorial Auditorium, three landmark structures on the university's historic "Forty Acres," were made by Mineral Wells Tile Company rather than Ludowici, which supplied most of the rest of UT's Spanish-tile roofs. The Main Building's tile, covering roughly 30,000 square feet including the President's Office, is a Mission-style profile known as Cubana. Because the company is long closed, UT now keeps a stockpile of original Mineral Wells tile at its J.J. Pickle Research Campus for future repairs rather than relying on new stock.

St. John's Anglican Church in Fort Worth, a Gothic Revival complex designed by church member and architect Joseph J. Patterson, also had a Mineral Wells shingle-tile roof. When hail badly damaged the roof on March 17, 2016, the church couldn't simply order replacement tile since Mineral Wells Tile Company was no longer in production. Instead, samples of the surviving tile, with its kiln-flashed range of buffs, browns, and reds, were sent to Ludowici, which custom-matched the profile, finish, and color to produce a compatible replacement.

## The Company's End and What's Left Today

Although the exact date of the company's closing is not known, what's clear is that its tile has outlived it in the way historic building materials often do: institutions like UT Austin stockpile the original stock rather than replace it, and when repairs are unavoidable, manufacturers like Ludowici are called in to reverse-engineer a match rather than simply substitute a different tile. For a company whose own paper trail is thin, that's a fairly durable kind of legacy.

The St. John's roof is the template for how these roofs get saved: a surviving tile becomes the reference for a hand-matched replacement, not the reason to strip the whole roof. Sourcing and matching discontinued Texas tile like Mineral Wells is [exactly what we do](discontinued.html) on historic roofs across the region.
