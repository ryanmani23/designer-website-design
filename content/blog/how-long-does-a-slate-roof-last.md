Slate outlives the people who install it. A roof quarried and laid correctly will still be on the house when the grandchildren inherit it. That is not a sales line. It is the reason slate has covered cathedrals and capitol buildings for centuries while the asphalt down the street gets torn off every fifteen years.

So the honest answer to how long a slate roof lasts is this: the slate itself will last 75 to 200 years depending on where it was quarried. Everything else on the roof will fail long before the slate does. And when a slate roof fails early, the slate is almost never the reason.

## The number depends on the stone, not the brand

Slate is not manufactured. It is farmed out of the ground, and the ground it comes from decides how long it lasts.

Hard Vermont and New York slates are the long-lived ones. A Vermont unfading grey or a Vermont black will commonly run 100 to 150 years, and many run longer. Pennsylvania soft-vein slate is a different stone with a different life, often closer to 75 to 100 years. Imported slates vary widely, and some of the cheaper ones on the market will not see 50.

This is why a roof is not just "slate." It matters which quarry, which seam, which grade. We work directly with quarry families who live where the stone comes out of the ground, because the difference between a slate that lasts a century and one that delaminates in your second decade is decided before the tile ever reaches the roof.

## What actually fails first

Walk a hundred failed slate roofs and you will find the same story over and over. The slate is fine. The roof failed anyway.

Here is what failed instead:

- **The flashings.** Slate roofs live and die at the valleys, the chimneys, the sidewalls, and every other place water has to change direction. Done in proper copper, those details outlast the homeowner. Done in painted steel or, worse, caulk and tar, they rot out in a decade and take the deck below them with it.
- **The fasteners.** Slate fastened with the wrong nails will release tiles one storm at a time. Copper or stainless is not optional on a roof meant to last a century. Electro-galvanized nails rust, and the roof slides off them.
- **The underlayment and deck.** These are the parts no one sees and the parts that determine whether the structure stays dry while the slate does its job.
- **The last guy.** More good slate has been ruined by careless foot traffic and bad repairs than by weather. A roofer who walks a slate roof like it is asphalt will crack more tiles than a hailstorm.

The pattern is always the same. The expensive, permanent part of the roof gets undone by the cheap, temporary parts around it, installed by someone who did not know the difference.

## Why so many slate roofs fail decades early

Slate punishes shortcuts. It is heavy, it is unforgiving, and it does not let you hide bad work under a warranty and a fresh coat of granules. A slate roof installed by a crew that learned on asphalt will look acceptable for a few years and then begin to come apart, and by then the crew is long gone.

The roofs that reach their full life have three things in common. The right stone for the climate. Copper at every transition. And an installer who treats the roof as a system meant to last a century, not a job to close out by Friday.

## In Dallas, the enemy is not age. It is hail and heat.

A Vermont slate does not care about a Texas summer. The freeze-thaw cycles that crack soft stone in the northeast are not the threat here. What we see in the DFW Metroplex is hail and thermal movement, and both of those are survivable when the roof is detailed correctly.

Hail can break individual slates. That is true of any roof. The difference is that a properly built slate roof is repairable tile by tile, indefinitely, by someone who knows how. You do not tear off a slate roof because a storm cracked forty tiles. You replace forty tiles and the roof goes back to outliving everyone.

## The real question to ask

"How long does a slate roof last" is the wrong question. The roof will last longer than you will. The right question is whether the roof on your house was built to let the slate do that, or built to look like slate until the warranty runs out.
