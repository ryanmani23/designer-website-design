A slate or tile roof is built to outlive its owners, but that does not mean it asks nothing of them. It means the maintenance is different from what most homeowners are used to, and most of what an estate roof needs in North Texas is restraint: knowing what to leave alone, what to watch, and who to let up there.

The climate here is not the one these roofs were originally engineered against. Slate and clay earned their reputation surviving northeastern winters, where the threat is water freezing and thawing inside the stone. That is not our problem. Our problems are hail, heat, wind, and the people who climb up after a storm. Here is how to think about each.

## Hail is the real threat, and it is survivable

Hail is the one weather event that can genuinely damage a slate, tile, or composite roof in this region. Large hail can crack individual slates and tiles. That is true of every roof material, including the ones that hide it better.

The important thing to understand is that a properly built estate roof is repairable, tile by tile, indefinitely. A storm that cracks forty slates is not a reason to tear off the roof. It is a reason to replace forty slates. The homes that end up needing full re-roofs after hail are usually the ones where a contractor convinced an owner that a repairable roof was a total loss, or where the original roof was built badly enough that the storm just exposed it.

After a hailstorm, the right move is an inspection by someone who knows the material, a documented assessment of what actually broke, and targeted repairs. Not a tear-off by the first crew that knocks on the door.

## A word on the crews that knock after a storm

North Texas hailstorms bring out storm-chasing contractors who work on volume and move on. They are very good at getting on roofs and very willing to call an estate roof totaled, because a tear-off and replacement is a bigger ticket than a repair.

An estate or historic roof should not be inspected, repaired, or assessed by anyone who treats slate like asphalt. More good slate and tile is destroyed by careless boots during post-storm inspections than by the storms themselves. Be selective about who you let on the roof, and be skeptical of anyone whose first conclusion is that the whole thing has to come off.

## Insurance, handled carefully

When hail does real damage, there may be a legitimate insurance claim, and there may not be. Much of it is a math question: the cost of your deductible against the cost and the purpose of the repairs.

We do not encourage filing a claim without genuine, documented cause that an insurer owes. What we do is assess the whole roof honestly, document what we find, and help you communicate clearly with your insurance so the decision is based on the actual condition of the roof rather than on whatever an adjuster managed to photograph from the ground.

## Heat and thermal movement

Texas heat does not hurt slate or clay, but it does move everything on the roof through large daily temperature swings. Over years, that movement is what works fasteners loose and opens up sealant joints if the roof was detailed with sealant where it should have had metal.

This is not something you manage from a ladder. It is something that gets handled correctly at installation, with copper flashings that accommodate movement and fasteners specified for a roof meant to last. If your roof relies on caulk at the transitions, the heat will find it. That is a reason for a proper assessment, not a maintenance chore.

## What an owner should actually do

The maintenance an estate roof genuinely needs is modest, and most of it is about catching small problems before they become deck problems.

- **Keep the valleys and gutters clear.** Debris that traps water is one of the few things that can defeat a well-built roof. This is the most useful routine thing an owner can stay on top of.
- **Look up after major storms,** ideally with binoculars from the ground, for slipped, cracked, or missing tiles. Note them. Do not climb up to investigate.
- **Have it inspected periodically by someone who knows the material,** not annually out of anxiety, but on a sensible interval and after significant hail or wind.
- **Address small repairs promptly.** A handful of slipped slates is a quick fix. The same problem ignored for three years lets water into the deck, and now it is a structural repair.
- **Stay off the roof,** and keep others off it. The single most effective thing most owners can do for a slate or tile roof is to limit who walks on it.

## The point

A well-built estate roof in North Texas is not high-maintenance. It is low-maintenance and unforgiving of the wrong attention. Keep it clear, watch it after storms, let only people who understand the material on it, and it will do exactly what it was built to do, which is last longer than you will.
