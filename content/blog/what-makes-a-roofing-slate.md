A roofing slate is not defined by appearance but by geology: it is a fine-grained, low-grade metamorphic rock whose metamorphic intensity sits in a narrow optimum where cleavage, strength and impermeability peak together. This article synthesizes the work of geologist Dr. Víctor Cárdenes Van den Eynde, whose research at the University of Oviedo and Ghent University produced the first petrographic classification of roofing slates, and whose seminar material frames slate quality in two parts: intrinsic features (mineralogy and internal fabric) and extrinsic features (environment and installation). Following Cárdenes, we trace slate from sediment to finished shingle, explain how metamorphic grade governs water absorption, flexural strength, brightness and splitting thickness, explain the chemistry of slate color, and review test data from more than 200 technical sheets that Cárdenes compiled against the European standard EN 12326. We close with implications for specifiers, contractors and insurance professionals in the United States, where ASTM C406 governs grading.

## 1. Introduction

The word "slate" is applied loosely in the market, to Brazilian metalutites, Chinese phyllites and even synthetic shingles, yet only a specific rock performs like slate on a roof for [a century or more](/blog/do-slate-roofs-really-last-100-years). Cárdenes has argued for years that this confusion is the root problem of the industry: as late as the TOMOSLATE project at Ghent University, he observed that no standard classification of roofing slate varieties existed anywhere ([CORDIS, TOMOSLATE](https://cordis.europa.eu/project/rcn/187735/brief/en)). His review "Petrography of roofing slates" (Cárdenes et al., 2014) and his critical review of the EN and ASTM standards (Cárdenes et al., 2016) set out to close that gap.

This article follows the structure Cárdenes uses when teaching architects and specifiers. Section 2 explains how slate forms and why its cleavage exists. Sections 3 and 4 show how metamorphic grade sorts rocks into metalutite, slate, phyllite and mica schist, and what that does to performance. Section 5 covers color. Sections 6 and 7 present his two-part quality model, intrinsic and extrinsic. Section 8 reviews his compiled test evidence, and Section 9 translates the findings for the U.S. market.

Unless otherwise noted, figures and interpretations are drawn from Cárdenes' seminar slides on roofing slate (sections 1.1 "What is a roofing slate?" and 1.3 "Quality: what makes a good roofing slate?") and his peer-reviewed papers listed in the References.

Our team at Priority Designer was privileged to take a class on this material with Dr. Cárdenes himself, the renowned slate geologist, while visiting the slate quarries in Spain from which we source the natural slate we sell and install. Much of what follows reflects that instruction, seen first-hand alongside the quarry faces and splitting sheds where these properties are put to the test.

## 2. From sediment to slate: cleavage development

Slate begins as mud. Fine clay and silt settle on the sea floor and compact into shale or mudstone, which Cárdenes calls by the geological term lutite. In this sedimentary stage the platy clay minerals lie loosely packed and randomly oriented, so the rock has no reliable plane along which to split.

The second stage is low-grade metamorphism. When a sedimentary basin is folded during mountain building, the mudrock is buried and squeezed at relatively low temperatures and pressures. Under that directed stress, as Cárdenes illustrates using Lutgens and Tarbuck (2016), the clay minerals recrystallize into chlorite and white mica (muscovite) and rotate into tight parallel alignment, perpendicular to the main compression.

That alignment is slaty cleavage. Because billions of microscopic mica and chlorite plates lie in the same plane, the rock splits along that plane into thin, flat, even sheets, independent of the original bedding. Cleavage is therefore the single property that makes a rock usable as a roofing slate, and everything else Cárdenes describes either supports or degrades it.

Geologists label these planes: S0 for the original sedimentary bedding and S1 for the metamorphic cleavage. The relationship between the two becomes important for strength, as Section 6 shows.

## 3. Metamorphic grade: four rocks sold as "slate"

Cárdenes' central teaching point is that the intensity of metamorphism creates the different varieties of roofing slate, and only one point on that scale yields true slate. The same parent mud, metamorphosed more or less intensely, becomes four commercially distinct rocks.

| Rock | Metamorphic grade | Fabric and appearance | Roofing behavior (after Cárdenes) |
| :- | :- | :- | :- |
| Metalutite | Lowest; barely past sedimentary | Weak, poorly developed cleavage; dull, slightly rough face | Splits thick and irregular; higher porosity; weak fabric (e.g., Brazilian metalutite) |
| Slate | Low (optimal) | Penetrative slaty cleavage; smooth, fine-grained face | Thin, flat, even splitting; peak strength; low absorption |
| Phyllite | Low-to-medium | Coarser micas give a satin sheen and crinkled (crenulated) surface | Sheen and texture; strength and splitting quality begin to decline |
| Mica schist | Medium-to-high | Visible mica crystals, wavy foliation, often stained | Irregular, thick, brittle pieces; poor dimensional regularity |

Cárdenes' photographs of finished shingles from each rock make the point visually: the true slate has a smooth, uniform face with crisp edges, while the phyllite is visibly crinkled and the mica schist is ragged and stained. The geological diagram he uses places all four along a single orogenic cross-section, with metamorphic conditions rising toward the core of the mountain belt.

The practical consequence is that "slate" in a product name is not proof of slate in the rock. Cárdenes' later work defined a formal International Roofing Slate Classification (IRSC), which sorts roofing slates into twelve lithotypes by petrographic characteristics and color (Cárdenes et al., 2020).

## 4. Technical properties and metamorphism

As metamorphic grade rises, crystal (grain) size increases, and Cárdenes shows that four key properties respond in different ways. Two of them peak at the slate grade and then fall, which is why true slate is an optimum rather than an extreme.

| Property | Trend from metalutite to mica schist | Mechanism (per Cárdenes) | Best performer |
| :- | :- | :- | :- |
| Water absorption | Falls steadily | The pore system closes as minerals recrystallize, leaving water less space to penetrate | Higher grades absorb least, but at a cost in strength |
| Flexural strength | Rises, plateaus at slate, then falls | Growing crystals make the rock more brittle past the optimum | Slate |
| Splitting thickness quality | Rises, peaks at slate, then falls | Depends on how well slaty cleavage develops, which is optimal in slate | Slate |
| Brightness | Rises steadily | Larger mica crystals reflect more light | Phyllite and schist (a cosmetic, not structural, gain) |

The lesson Cárdenes draws is that no single property defines quality. A phyllite may absorb little water and shine attractively, yet be weaker and split poorly. A metalutite may split, but its open pore system and weak fabric undermine durability. Only slate combines low absorption, high flexural strength and thin, regular splitting.

This matches his earlier quantitative work linking mineralogy to modulus of rupture in Iberian slates (Cárdenes et al., 2010, "Mineralogy and modulus of rupture of roofing slate") and his study of how composition controls color and brightness (Cárdenes et al., 2012).

## 5. Color: a record of ancient seawater oxygen

Slate color is set long before metamorphism, by how much oxygen was available on the sea floor when the mud was deposited. Cárdenes groups roofing slates into three color families, consistent with National Slate Association practice: Black-Gray (B), Green (G) and Red-Purple (R).

The major minerals, quartz, mica and chlorite, are largely the same across families. The difference lies in accessory components, which make up less than about 2% of the rock:

| Color family | Coloring accessory minerals | Depositional condition |
| :- | :- | :- |
| Red-Purple (R) | Iron oxides (hematite) | Oxic: oxygen available in the water and sediment |
| Green (G) | Iron sulphides (pyrite); minor organic carbon | Anoxic just below the sediment surface, with modest organic input |
| Black-Gray (B) | Organic matter (carbon) and iron sulphides (pyrite) | Strongly anoxic, with an overload of organic sedimentation |

Cárdenes illustrates this with three sea-floor scenarios. Under normal organic sedimentation, a thin red oxidized layer forms above green clay. With enhanced organic input, oxygen is used up at the surface and the whole deposit is green, with iron sulphides forming. With an overload of organic matter, the water itself becomes oxygen-free and black clay accumulates, rich in carbon and pyrite.

This matters for durability as well as aesthetics. The same anoxic chemistry that makes black slates black also supplies the iron sulphides whose behavior on the roof Cárdenes examines under extrinsic quality (Section 7). His micro-CT work in the TOMOSLATE project even revealed populations of framboidal pyrite inside slates ([CORDIS](https://cordis.europa.eu/article/id/198037-new-insight-into-natural-slate-solves-roofing-markets-problems/en)).

## 6. Intrinsic quality: what the rock brings to the roof

Cárdenes defines intrinsic features as properties fixed by geology, which no manufacturer or installer can change. There are two: mineralogical composition and internal structure, or fabric.

### 6.1 Mineralogical composition

A typical good roofing slate in Cárdenes' model is dominated by sheet silicates (phyllosilicates) plus quartz:

| Mineral | Typical share | Role |
| :- | :- | :- |
| Muscovite (white mica) | ~35% | Aligned plates create cleavage and flexibility |
| Chlorite | ~30% | Aligned plates; contributes green and gray tones |
| Quartz | ~25% | Hardness and resistance to wear |
| Feldspar | ~8% | Framework mineral |
| Accessory minerals | ~2% | Color and, potentially, weathering behavior |

These main minerals, Cárdenes stresses, are stable under normal environmental conditions, which is the geological basis of slate's century-scale service life. The caveat is the accessory fraction. Iron sulphides and carbonates can occasionally be present, always below about 5%, and under certain circumstances they can alter. This is why rigorous producers and standards test for them rather than assume their absence.

### 6.2 Internal structure: fabric

Metamorphism creates a solid internal structure that gives good slate its exceptional flexural strength. Cárdenes contrasts two micrographs: a Brazilian metalutite, whose low metamorphic grade leaves a weak, poorly organized fabric with discontinuous laminae, and a Del Carmen slate from Spain, whose optimal grade produces strong, closely spaced cleavage planes.

### 6.3 Hebra: the slate's internal reinforcement

The most distinctive concept in Cárdenes' teaching is the hebra, a Spanish quarry term he translates as grain. The hebra is the line where the original sedimentary bedding (S0) intersects the metamorphic cleavage (S1). Where the two planes cross, they form a woven internal structure that greatly increases the slate's bending resistance.

Cárdenes uses a structural-engineering analogy. Plain concrete under a point load cracks and fails at mid-span, while reinforced concrete flexes because steel bars carry the tension. In a well-formed slate, the hebra acts like those reinforcing bars. This is one reason slates are cut with their long dimension oriented relative to the grain: strength depends on direction, which is also why both EN 12326 and ASTM C406 test bending in more than one orientation.

Note on terminology: in U.S. trade usage, "grain" usually refers to the direction along which slate breaks at right angles to cleavage. Cárdenes' hebra describes the same structural feature from a geologist's perspective, so readers should confirm which meaning a specification intends.

## 7. Extrinsic quality: environment and installation

Extrinsic features, in Cárdenes' framework, are conditions imposed on the slate after it leaves the quarry. Even a geologically excellent slate can underperform if these are ignored.

### 7.1 Environmental conditions

Inside the rock massif, Cárdenes explains, conditions are non-oxidizing and all minerals are in balance; pyrite, pyrrhotite and carbonates are stable. Quarrying, manufacturing, transport and installation move the slate into oxidizing conditions. On the roof, urban gas emissions and acid rain create an acidic environment that triggers weathering.

He identifies three typical responses. Surface carbonates dissolve and disappear. Carbonates inside the slate can react with sulphur-bearing acid to form gypsum (gypsification), which occupies more volume than the original mineral and can stress the surrounding rock. Iron sulphides are usually stable, but some may oxidize, producing rust staining and, where the sulphide is the unstable variety or poorly distributed, local deterioration.

This is the mechanism behind the thermal-cycle, sulphur dioxide exposure and oxidation tests in EN 12326 and the weathering tests in ASTM C406. Cárdenes' 2002 work with García-Guinea and colleagues specifically addressed determining iron sulphides in northwest Spanish slates, and his standards review (Cárdenes et al., 2016) argues these tests can be optimized to give more useful information.

### 7.2 Adequate installation

Cárdenes is blunt on this point: roofing slate installation is an art, and if the slater is not a skilled professional the result may be defective, and it is not the slate's fault. His seminar pairs a straight, evenly coursed and properly lapped roof with a roof showing irregular coursing, mixed and discolored units and failing details around penetrations.

For contractors, this places headlap, fastening, flashing and coursing alongside geology as determinants of service life. A premium slate cannot compensate for inadequate headlap or corroding nails.

## 8. Test evidence: EN 12326 data from over 200 technical sheets

Cárdenes compiled bending-strength and water-absorption results from more than 200 producer technical sheets tested under EN 12326, and the data show that most commercial slates clear the standard's floors comfortably, with a long tail of weaker products. Values below are read from his box plots and are approximate; strength is assumed to be in MPa and absorption in percent by mass.

| Measure (EN 12326) | Median | Middle 50% of products | Typical range (whiskers) | Notable outliers |
| :- | :- | :- | :- | :- |
| Bending strength, series 1 (MPa) | ~51 | ~44–57 | ~27–74 | One product near 84 |
| Bending strength, series 2 (MPa) | ~41 | ~35–47 | ~19–60 | One near 67; one near 1, below the lower limit |
| Water absorption (%) | ~0.22 | ~0.17–0.28 | ~0.06–0.46 | Several between 0.52 and 0.68 |

The two bending series correspond to different test orientations relative to the grain, which the slide does not label. The consistently lower second series reflects the directional strength Cárdenes attributes to fabric and hebra (Section 6). His chart marks a lower limit of roughly 15 MPa; one product fell far below it.

Cárdenes positions three Spanish producers, GALCAR, Franvisa and Del Castillo, within these distributions, and their spread illustrates his point that orientation matters. In the first series GALCAR (~74) and Franvisa (~71) sit near the top and Del Castillo (~51) at the median. In the second series GALCAR stays high (~56) while Del Castillo (~31) and Franvisa (~25) fall into the lower quartile. All three show absorption of roughly 0.27–0.31%, slightly above the sample median but well within the middle range.

Two cautions apply. First, a single averaged value hides directional and within-quarry variation, a limitation Cárdenes criticizes in his standards review (Cárdenes et al., 2016). Second, EN 12326 and ASTM C406 use different specimens and thresholds, so European results cannot be read directly against U.S. grade limits. Under ASTM C406, for example, Grade S1 requires water absorption of no more than 0.25%, and S2 and S3 allow 0.36% and 0.45% respectively. By that yardstick, roughly half of the products in Cárdenes' sample would meet the S1 absorption limit, subject to confirmation by ASTM testing.

## 9. Implications for specifiers, contractors and adjusters

Cárdenes' framework translates into a short set of practical rules for anyone specifying, installing or evaluating slate in the United States.

**Specify the rock, not the word.** Require natural slate graded under ASTM C406 (S1 for a 75-plus-year service life), with test reports for absorption (ASTM C121), modulus of rupture (ASTM C120) and weathering (ASTM C217). Under C406, S1 slate must show modulus of rupture of at least 9,000 psi across the grain and 6,000 psi along it. Treat metalutites and phyllites as different materials, not lower grades of slate.

**Ask about accessory minerals.** Because Cárdenes locates most durability risk in the small accessory fraction, request petrographic or oxidation-test data on iron sulphides and carbonates, especially for black slates destined for polluted urban environments.

**Respect direction.** Strength is directional. Confirm that units are cut with the correct grain orientation, and expect lower values in one test direction.

**Hold installation to the same standard as the stone.** Cárdenes' extrinsic model makes the slater a quality factor. Proper headlap for the slope, copper or stainless fasteners, and correctly detailed flashings are as important as the quarry.

**For storm and insurance evaluation.** The geology supports two defensible points. First, slate's strength comes from its fabric, so a fractured or delaminated unit has lost the structure that gave it its rated performance and cannot be restored by patching. Second, matching is a geological question: color family, accessory mineralogy and brightness come from a specific deposit (Sections 5 and 6), so replacement units from a different quarry or rock type may differ in color, weathering behavior and sheen. These are reasoned applications of Cárdenes' framework, not claims he makes about claims practice, and should be presented that way.

## 10. Conclusion

What makes a roofing slate, in Víctor Cárdenes' account, is a precise geological history: marine mud of the right composition, colored by the oxygen conditions of its sea floor, metamorphosed just enough to align its micas into penetrative cleavage and not so much that its crystals coarsen into brittle phyllite or schist. That optimum yields the combination no other rock offers: thin, flat splitting; low water absorption; high, directionally reinforced flexural strength; and minerals stable for centuries.

Quality then depends on what the rock brings (mineralogy and fabric) and what the world does to it (environment and installation). Cárdenes' contribution has been to turn that understanding into classification, petrography and better testing, giving specifiers and contractors a scientific basis for distinguishing true slate from the many materials sold under its name.
