In a world littered with commodification and manufacturing, Slate is one of the few materials that remains a completely natural resource, almost entirely unmanipulated aside from being cut and split. As is true with many things in life, the history of slate as a roofing material is more complicated and more interesting than one would typically think. Slate, much like oil, is a natural resource that can be found throughout the earth and the first place we know that it was found, mined, and applied to roofs is in Ancient Rome.

Pliny the Elder in his encyclopedia *Naturalis Historia*, which he finished around 77AD, wrote that there were quarries in Liguria, in the North West region of Italy that contained<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1">1</a></sup>, “soft and flexible stones…obtained when extracted, but they soon become hard.”<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-2">2</a></sup>

Yet, the Roman Empire was vast and much of the slate Rome used came from the region today that would be modern day Northern Wales. For example, the same year Pliny the Elder finished his encyclopedia, the Roman fort of Segontium was established by Gnaeus Julius Agricola. And while Segontium was originally roofed with clay tile, around a century later in the 2nd century AD, it was reroofed and floored with slate. Most of the slates were rounded/diamond shapes with one hole for nailing as can be seen in the below figure. Though later, a standardized method for roofing slate was set in place by the Romans, in Ceredigion, Southern Wales, which cut the slates to dimensions of 30×30 cm (~12x12 inches) and thicknesses greater than one centimeter.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1">1</a></sup>

When the Romans left the Isles, it was over half a millennium until we heard activity of slate roofing again, which came in the Medieval age. Between the 11-13th centuries, slate mining became a true industry thanks to the Normans.

In the late 13th century, King Edward I recorded during the Welsh Wars of Independence that he slept under a slate roof. Insignificant as this detail may seem, it was notable for King Edward I and has since been for historians, showing that even higher class was honored to have such a roof. In fact, most of the slate that was quarried during this period belonged to the Church, the nobility, and large landowners, who used the scarce production for their own civil and religious buildings.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1">1</a></sup> Because of its exclusivity, slate was primarily utilized for unique roofing projects, which cemented its status as a high-end building material and a symbol of wealth and social standing (Lariviere 1986). While the rising middle class sought to use slate for their own homes, it came at a significant cost. Within pre-industrial mining, the vast majority of the labor force (approximately 80%) focused on extraction and block-cutting within the mine itself. The raw stone was then passed to processing teams consisting of two to six people. These workers operated in specialized roles, which involved splitting and exfoliating the stone, tracing the surface according to a pattern, and shaping the final pieces with a machete or axe (Gwyn 2015).

The industrial revolution of the 18th century helped boom the slate industry into what we know it now to be. The UK was first, opening quarries all throughout and exporting by boat to whoever would buy. It was in the 19th century that as emigrants flooded into Ellis Island from Europe, slaters came from the UK, Wales, France, Germany and are the reason America discovered slate quarries, many of which still produce today. The role of European immigration in the North American slate industry is reflected in the following aphorism: “It takes a Welshman to find slate, a Pennsylvanian Dutchman to dig it up but a Czech to make any money out of it!”<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1">1</a></sup>

This all brings us to the present day. Currently, Spain is the largest producer of slate in the world, accounting for ~90% of all the slate that is produced. It was the French who showed the Spanish how to develop and export its wealth of natural slate, most of which is found in the Galicia region of Northern Spain. Second to Spain in production would be the USA, namely the Vermont slate valley. While slates in America have also come from Pennsylvania, Virginia, New York, and California, almost all current production comes out of a small stretch of quarries in Vermont. Other countries have begun to produce and sell slate as well, such as China, India, Canada, Argentina, and Brazil. But the highest regarded, highest rated slate is from Spain or Vermont.

Before finishing, I believe a natural question that arises in all of this is: why should anyone care? And while not everyone needs to, we at Priority Designer believe we only have a short amount of time on this earth; and one of the things that enriches that time is our connection to the world around us. Slate is a product pulled directly from the earth, cut, split and installed by hands across two thousand years, that can protect us and the homes we live our lives in. There’s something beautiful in choosing a material that connects us to both the ground beneath us and the people who came before us: ancient Romans, Kings, peasants, and immigrants alike.
