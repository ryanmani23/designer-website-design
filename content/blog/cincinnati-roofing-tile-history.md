By the 1890s, Ohio was already becoming the center of American roofing tile manufacture, and it stayed that way for decades. Amid a wave of Ohio tile plants that opened and folded within a few years of each other, one company built in the Cincinnati area managed to do what most of its contemporaries could not: last.

## A German Immigrant's Plant at Winton Place

The Cincinnati Roofing Tile and Terra Cotta Co. was founded in 1895 at Winton Place, a community just outside Cincinnati, by Jacob Freund. Freund was one of many German immigrants of the era whose personal appreciation for a well-made tile roof, a tradition carried over from home, translated directly into a business.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1">1</a></sup>

That tradition ran deep. Cincinnati itself was one point of the so-called "German Triangle," along with Milwaukee and St. Louis, three cities that drew especially heavy German settlement through the 19th century. Germans had been arriving in Cincinnati since 1788, but the real wave began in the 1830s and accelerated sharply after the failed 1848 revolutions in the German states sent a generation of "forty-eighters" across the Atlantic, many of whom settled in Cincinnati's Over-the-Rhine district and went on to shape the city's business, civic, and cultural life. By the time Freund built his plant in 1895, German heritage wasn't a minor detail of Cincinnati's character, it was close to half the city's identity.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-2">2</a></sup>

The tile tradition Freund brought with him wasn't incidental, either. Roofing tile has a documented history of following settlers rather than being invented fresh in a new country: the 1910 Ohio Geological Survey account that records Freund's founding also notes that colonies "usually adhere to the form of roofing tile in use in their parent countries," pointing to German Pennsylvania settlers making flat tile as early as 1735, and to the Zoarites, a German religious community in Tuscarawas County, Ohio, who built an entire town on tile roofs they knew how to make because they'd learned it before they ever left Germany. Freund's plant, arriving decades later in a different corner of the state, fits the same pattern, a German immigrant applying an inherited building tradition to American soil rather than starting from nothing.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1">1</a></sup>

Freund started modestly and built the company up step by step with his associates, rather than launching at scale from the outset. Beyond his name, his location, and his motivation as described in the 1910 survey, I haven't been able to find further biographical detail on Freund himself, his birth year, immigration date, or life outside the company.

## Succeeding Where Others Failed

The mid-1890s were a crowded, difficult time to start an Ohio tile plant. The Repp Roofing Tile Co. of New Philadelphia and the Barnard Tile Co. of Bellaire both opened around 1893; the Zanesville Roofing Tile Co. opened the same year as Cincinnati Roofing Tile and Terra Cotta, in 1895; another plant followed at Ottawa in 1900. None of these operated more than three or four years. Cincinnati Roofing Tile and Terra Cotta was the exception. According to a 1910 Ohio Geological Survey account of the industry, the company solved its manufacturing problems independently, much as the pioneering Akron plant had, and produced tile of a different shape and design from its competitors rather than copying an existing product. That combination, working out its own methods instead of imitating someone else's, was credited as the reason it succeeded where the others quietly disappeared.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1">1</a></sup>

The company's name also points to a broader scope than roofing tile alone. Unlike single-purpose tile works, Cincinnati Roofing Tile and Terra Cotta made architectural terra cotta as well, placing it in the same category as firms that supplied both roofing material and decorative building elements from one plant. Its products included a Spanish-style profile known as the "True S" tile, a four-corner tile (not a clipped-corner design) in a documented size still referenced today in the restoration trade at roughly 12 1/2 by 9 1/2 inches.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-3">3</a></sup>

## Part of an Ohio Boom

By the time the Ohio Geological Survey's report was written, Ohio was producing roughly a third of all roofing tile made in the United States, more than any other state, and Cincinnati Roofing Tile and Terra Cotta was one of only a handful of Ohio plants still active after the shakeout of the 1890s. That survival alone made it noteworthy: of the wave of small Ohio tile companies founded in the years just before and after 1895, most were gone within half a decade.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1">1</a></sup>

## What's Left Today

Documentation of the company's later years, including when it eventually closed, is thin. What's clear is that it earned a place in the industry's own written history at the time, a level of recognition many of its short-lived Ohio contemporaries never got, precisely because it did things differently rather than following the templates already failing around it.

The tile still turns up in service, too. We recently replaced Cincinnati "four-corner S" tile on a roof in St. Louis after the 2025 tornado, a reminder that a company gone for the better part of a century can still have its tile protecting a home, and can still [need matching](discontinued.html) when a storm takes a few tiles out.
